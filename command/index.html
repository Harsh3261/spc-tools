ISO 9001 Audit Guide

Your QMS and the external audit, explained plainly

Start here

What an external audit is, which one comes next for you, and what your job is on the day.

Your next audit: surveillance audit 1

You were recertified this year, so a new three-year cycle has started. Next comes a surveillance audit, about a year after recertification, then a second one the year after, then recertification again in year 3.

A surveillance audit is shorter than a recertification audit. The auditor samples part of the system and doesn't go through every clause. But some areas get checked at every surveillance audit (next card).

You already do half of what the auditor checks

You haven't been through an external audit before, but the work behind most findings is work you already do every day:

What's new for you is the system-level part: management review, internal audit programme, objectives, risks and context. Spend most of your prep time there. Then use the Drill tab to practise.

What gets checked at every surveillance audit

These come from the rules certification bodies work to (ISO/IEC 17021-1). Have them ready no matter what else is on the plan:

  1. Findings from this year's recertification: were they really fixed, and did the fix work? This is usually checked first.
  2. Internal audits: done as planned, with findings raised and followed up.
  3. Management review: held, with the required inputs and decisions recorded.
  4. Customer complaints and how you handled them.
  5. Quality objectives: are you hitting them, and what are you doing where you aren't?
  6. Continual improvement: actual improvements, with evidence.
  7. Changes since the last audit: people (including your new role), processes, equipment, products, sites.
  8. Use of the certificate and logo on your website, quotes and letterhead.

Plus whichever processes the audit plan lists for this visit, such as production, purchasing or calibration.

What an external audit is

A certification body (CB, sometimes called a registrar) sends an auditor to check two things: that your quality management system (QMS) meets ISO 9001, and that people actually follow it. In Canada, CBs are usually accredited by SCC or another accreditation body that's a member of the IAF.

The auditor samples: a few records, a few jobs, a few people. Then they follow trails, for example from a customer PO through to the drawing, the inspection record, the gauge used and that gauge's calibration certificate.

The auditor isn't there to inspect parts or to fail you. Their job is to report what the evidence shows, good or bad. A calm, organised site with records that are easy to find makes for a short, easy audit.

The three questions behind every audit question

1
Do you have it? Is the requirement covered by a process, procedure or plan?
2
Do you do it? Do records show it happening, every time and not just sometimes?
3
Does it work? Do results show the process is effective: objectives met, problems not coming back?

Shop-floor version: say what you do, do what you say, prove it, and show it's working.

Your role as management rep

ISO 9001:2015 doesn't require a job titled "management representative". It requires top management to assign responsibilities (clause 5.3), including reporting on how the QMS is performing. In practice, the auditor will treat you as the main contact. Top management still owns the QMS (5.1.1). Expect the auditor to interview them directly, so they need to be ready too.

On audit day you:

  • Agree the audit plan and timings with the auditor, and make sure the right people are there.
  • Act as guide, or assign a guide who stays with the auditor the whole time.
  • Keep a request log: every record asked for, who got it, and what was shown.
  • Watch for possible findings as they come up, and bring evidence before the closing meeting.
  • Accept the findings professionally and own the response afterwards.

ISO 9001:2026: what's coming

ISO 9001:2026 was published on 16 September 2026. Certificates to the 2015 edition stay valid during the three-year transition, which ends on 30 September 2029. After that date, 2015 certificates expire.

It updates the 2015 edition rather than replacing it, and the changes are moderate. The main ones:

  • Quality culture and ethical behaviour are now stated in leadership (5.1.1) and awareness (7.3).
  • Risks and opportunities are split into separate requirements for identifying and addressing each (6.1).
  • Planning of changes is expanded (6.3).
  • Climate change must be considered in context and interested parties (4.1/4.2). This came from the 2024 amendment, so you may already have it covered.
  • Continual improvement (10.1) is reworded and strengthened, core terms are now defined in the standard itself, and Annex A gives much more explanatory guidance.

Ask your CB whether they plan to do your transition at surveillance 1 or later, and which edition your next audit will be against. Clauses changed in 2026 are tagged 2026 in the Clauses tab.

Words you'll hear

Objective evidence
Something the auditor can see: a record, a document, an observation, or what someone says and can back up. Your opinion isn't evidence.
Audit criteria
What you're audited against: ISO 9001, your own procedures, customer requirements, and laws and regulations that apply.
Audit trail
Following one job or item through linked records to see whether the system holds together.
Nonconformity (NC)
A requirement not met. Graded major or minor (see Findings).
OFI / observation
Opportunity for improvement. Not a nonconformity, and no formal response required.
Correction
Fixing the immediate problem, e.g. recalibrating the overdue gauge.
Corrective action
Removing the root cause so the problem doesn't happen again.
Documented information
"Maintain" means a controlled document (procedure, instruction). "Retain" means a record (proof something happened).
Scope
What your certificate covers: products, activities, sites. Any clause you say doesn't apply, such as 8.3 design, must be justified (4.3).
Interested parties
People and groups who affect or are affected by your QMS: customers, suppliers, employees, regulators, owners.
Concession
Formal permission to use or release product that doesn't meet requirements. Usually this is what your deviation approval is (8.7).
CB / AB
Certification body (audits you) and accreditation body (audits the CB, e.g. SCC, ANAB).

Prepare

What to do from about two months out through the closing meeting, and after it.

6–8 weeks before

  • Get the audit plan from your CB. It gives the dates, auditor, scope, edition (2015 or 2026) and which processes and departments they'll visit. If it hasn't arrived, ask for it.
  • Re-read the recertification report. For every finding, check that the correction is done, the root cause is recorded, the corrective action is in place, and you have evidence it worked. This is usually the first thing checked.
  • Internal audits. Check that this year's audits were done as scheduled and cover the processes due. Findings should be raised and moving. If an area is behind, audit it now. An honest late audit is better than a gap.
  • Management review. Has one been held since recertification, or is one due? Check that the minutes cover every required input and record decisions (see 9.3 in Clauses).
  • Corrective action / NCR log. Nothing should be overdue without a documented reason and a new date. Old open items get noticed.
  • Tell the CB about changes: new management rep (you), reorganisation, new processes or equipment, scope changes. You're normally required to report significant changes.

2–4 weeks before

  • Calibration. Every gauge in use is in date and labelled. Anything found out of tolerance has a recorded check of the parts it measured (7.1.5.2).
  • Documents at point of use. Walk the floor. Drawings and work instructions at each station should be the current revision, with no uncontrolled printouts or handwritten notes acting as instructions.
  • Competence. Training matrix up to date, including new hires, CNC operators and inspectors. Records show they're qualified for what they do.
  • Suppliers. Approved supplier list, performance data and re-evaluations. Include outsourced special processes (heat treat, nitriding, plating) and material certs.
  • Quality objectives. Each one has a target, an owner, current data, and an action plan if it's off track.
  • Customers. Complaints logged with responses, and customer satisfaction data actually analysed.
  • Risks and opportunities. Reviewed this year and linked to real actions.
  • Certificate and logo. Check the website, quotes and email signatures for correct use.

The final week

  • Brief the people likely to be interviewed: supervisors, inspectors, operators, purchasing, shipping. Go over how to answer (Interview tab). Don't script them.
  • Walk the shop. Nonconforming and quarantined parts labelled and kept apart, every part's status clear, the area tidy.
  • Set up an audit folder, digital or paper: org chart, process map, scope, quality policy, objectives with data, management review minutes, internal audit schedule and reports, CAPA log, calibration list, approved supplier list, recertification report and closure evidence.
  • Logistics. Book a room, arrange PPE for the auditor, get screen access to your ERP and records, and name a runner to fetch records so you can stay with the auditor.
  • Top management has the opening and closing meetings and their interview slot in their calendar.

Audit day

1
Opening meeting (15–30 min). Introductions. The auditor confirms scope, plan, timings, confidentiality and how findings will be reported. Ask anything you're unsure of here, for example "Will you tell us about possible findings at the end of each day?"
2
The audit. Interviews, shop-floor tours and record checks, process by process. The guide stays with the auditor and the runner fetches records. Log every request.
3
Quick fixes. If something small is wrong, fix it on the spot, such as replacing an obsolete drawing. That's a correction. It shows good faith, but the auditor may still write it up, because the question is why it happened.
4
Daily wrap-up. Most auditors summarise their concerns at the end of each day. This is when to bring evidence you couldn't find earlier.
5
Closing meeting. Findings are read out with the clause and the evidence. Ask for clarification and present evidence you have, but don't argue. Confirm response deadlines and the auditor's recommendation.

After the audit

  • Log each finding in your corrective action system the same day, with an owner.
  • Send your response by the CB's deadline. See Findings for how to write one.
  • Schedule an effectiveness check for each finding. The next auditor will ask about it.
  • Present the results at the next management review, where they're a required input.
  • Write down what you'd do differently next time while it's fresh.

What the auditor checks

ISO 9001 clauses 4–10 in plain language: what the auditor asks, what to have ready, and what typically gets written up in a machining shop. Numbering follows the 2015 edition you're certified to.

4 Context5 Leadership6 Planning7 Support8 Operation9 Performance10 Improvement

4 · Context of the organization

4.1 4.2Context and interested parties 2026

Know the internal and external issues that affect your ability to deliver quality (market, competition, workforce, technology, regulations, climate), and who your interested parties are and what they need from you.

Auditor asks

"What are the main issues affecting the business? How do you keep this up to date? Where do they show up in your risks or objectives?"

Have ready

A context / interested-parties list (often in the management review or a SWOT), reviewed in the last year.

Common finding

The list was written years ago and never reviewed, or it doesn't connect to the risks and objectives.

2026 Climate change must be considered. That came from the 2024 amendment, so check it's already in your list.

4.3Scope of the QMS

What the system covers: products, activities, sites. A clause can be marked "not applicable" only if it truly doesn't affect product conformity, and you must give the reason.

Auditor asks

"Does the scope still describe what you do? You say design (8.3) doesn't apply. Who designs the cylinders?"

Common finding

The scope says "build to print", but the shop actually does design or engineering changes for customers.

4.4QMS and its processes

Your processes are defined: inputs, outputs, sequence, owners, measures, resources, risks.

Have ready

A process map or "turtle diagrams" showing how quoting → order review → purchasing → machining → inspection → shipping connect.

Common finding

A process has no measure of whether it works, or the map doesn't match reality.

5 · Leadership

5.1Leadership and customer focus 2026

Top management is accountable for the QMS, not just the quality department. They make sure policy and objectives fit the business strategy, resources are provided, and customer requirements and satisfaction stay a priority.

Auditor asks top management

"How are you involved in the QMS? What are your quality objectives and how are they doing? What did the last management review decide? How do you know customers are happy?"

Common finding

A manager can't talk about the objectives or results, which suggests the QMS belongs only to Quality.

2026 Leaders must promote a quality culture and ethical behaviour. Expect questions like "How would someone raise a quality concern? What happens when schedule and quality conflict?"

5.2Quality policy

A policy that fits your business, commits to meeting requirements and to improving, is communicated, and is understood.

Auditor asks an operator

"What does the quality policy mean for your job?" They don't need to recite it. A good answer in their own words: "Make it to the drawing, check it, and stop and tell someone if it's wrong."

5.3Roles, responsibilities and authorities

People know who's responsible for what, including who can stop production, release product and approve a deviation.

Have ready

Org chart, job descriptions, and the authority list (who approves concessions and deviations, who releases product).

Common finding

A deviation was signed by someone without the authority your procedure gives.

6 · Planning

6.1Risks and opportunities 2026

Identify what could stop you delivering quality (and what could help), take action in proportion to the risk, and check the actions worked. ISO 9001 doesn't require a formal FMEA or a risk register, but you need to show you did it.

Auditor asks

"What are your top risks? Show me one action from the risk review and how you know it worked."

Common finding

A risk register exists but is never reviewed, or the "actions" column is empty.

2026 Risks and opportunities are treated separately, so they'll need separate entries.

6.2Quality objectives

Measurable objectives (e.g. on-time delivery, scrap %, customer returns) with a plan: what will be done, resources, who, by when, and how results will be evaluated.

Auditor asks

"Show me the data. This one is red. What are you doing about it?"

Common finding

An objective is missed every month and nothing is done, or there's no data at all.

6.3Planning of changes 2026

Changes to the QMS (new process, new manager, new ERP, moving equipment) are planned: purpose, consequences, resources, and responsibilities.

Auditor asks

"What has changed since the last audit? How did you plan it?" Your own appointment as manager is an obvious example.

2026 Expanded: changes should also be communicated, monitored and reviewed for effectiveness.

7 · Support

7.1People, infrastructure, work environment

Enough people, maintained machines and facilities, and a suitable work environment.

Have ready

Preventive maintenance schedule and records for CNC machines, test benches and cranes.

Common finding

PM overdue on a critical machine, or a breakdown with no record.

7.1.5Monitoring and measuring resources (calibration)

Gauges and instruments used to verify product are suitable, calibrated at set intervals against traceable standards, identified with their status, and protected from damage or adjustment. If a gauge is found out of tolerance, you must check whether earlier measurements are still valid and act on the product they measured.

Auditor does

Picks a gauge off a bench, reads the ID, and asks to see its calibration record and certificate. They may also ask for a personal tool or a CMM probe qualification.

Have ready

Gauge list with due dates, certificates showing traceability (e.g. ISO/IEC 17025 lab), out-of-tolerance investigations, and the rule for personal or employee-owned tools.

Common finding

An overdue gauge in use; personal tools not controlled; an out-of-tolerance result with no product impact assessment; a CMM with no record of its verification.

7.1.6Organizational knowledge

Knowledge needed to run processes (setups, tricks for a difficult bore, lessons learned) is captured and shared so it doesn't leave when someone retires.

Auditor asks

"If your best setup person left tomorrow, how would you know how to run this job?"

7.2 7.3Competence and awareness 2026

People are competent (education, training, experience), you act on gaps and check that training worked, and records are kept. Everyone knows the policy, the objectives that affect them, how they contribute, and what happens if they don't follow the system.

Auditor does

Picks an operator and asks for their training record, then checks it covers the job they're doing right now.

Common finding

Training matrix missing the new hire; no evidence the training was effective; inspector signing off a CMM program without recorded CMM training.

2026 Awareness now includes quality culture and ethical behaviour.

7.4Communication

You decide what gets communicated, when, to whom and how, e.g. shift meetings, quality alerts, KPI boards.

7.5Documented information (documents and records)

Documents are reviewed, approved, revision-controlled and available at the point of use, and obsolete versions are removed or marked. Records are legible, protected, retained for a defined time, and retrievable.

Auditor does

Compares the drawing at the machine with the latest revision in the system. Asks how a revised drawing gets to the floor and how the old one is pulled.

Common finding

Old drawing revision at a workstation; uncontrolled printout with handwritten changes; records with no retention rule; forms filled in pencil or with blanks.

8 · Operation

8.1Operational planning and control

For each job, the requirements, acceptance criteria, resources and records needed are planned (router, control plan, inspection plan).

8.2Customer requirements and order review

Before you accept an order, you confirm you can meet it: drawing revision, specs, quantities, dates, special requirements. Changes to an order are reviewed and communicated.

Auditor does

Picks a recent PO and asks for evidence it was reviewed, then checks that the drawing revision on the PO matches the one used in production.

Common finding

No evidence of contract review; PO revision differs from the shop drawing; customer spec referenced on the PO but not flowed down to the floor or suppliers.

8.3Design and development

Applies only if you design products. If you do, you need planning, inputs, reviews, verification, validation, outputs and change control. If you've declared it not applicable, be ready to justify that (see 4.3).

8.4Purchasing and external providers

Suppliers, including outsourced processes, are selected, monitored and re-evaluated against criteria. POs give clear requirements: specs, revisions, certs needed, special process requirements. Incoming product is verified.

Auditor does

Picks a supplier, e.g. your nitriding or plating house, and asks for the evaluation, performance data and what the PO told them. Picks a material lot and asks for the mill cert.

Common finding

Supplier not on the approved list; never re-evaluated; PO missing the spec or the cert requirement; mill cert not checked against the spec.

8.5.1Control of production

Work is done under controlled conditions: instructions, suitable equipment and gauges, competent people, inspection at the right stages. Special processes (where the result can't be fully checked afterwards, e.g. welding, heat treatment, nitriding, plating) must be validated and periodically revalidated.

Auditor does

Stands at a machine and asks the operator what they're making, how they know it's right, and what they check and how often.

Common finding

First-piece inspection not recorded; in-process checks missed; special process never validated, or the supplier's validation never requested.

8.5.2Identification and traceability

Product is identified throughout production, its inspection status is clear, and where traceability is required (customer, heat or lot), it's recorded.

Auditor does

Picks a part off a rack: "What is this, what job, has it been inspected, and which material heat?"

Common finding

Untagged parts; status unclear (passed? waiting? rejected?); traceability chain broken.

8.5.3 8.5.4Customer property and preservation

Customer-owned items (material, tooling, gauges, drawings) are identified and protected, and loss or damage is reported to the customer. Product is protected through handling, storage, packaging and shipping: rust prevention, plugged ports, protected rod surfaces.

8.5.5 8.5.6Post-delivery and control of changes

Warranty, returns and field issues are handled. Unplanned production changes (substitute machine, alternate material, new program) are reviewed and authorised, and the review is recorded.

8.6Release of products

Product isn't shipped until planned checks are complete (unless the customer authorises otherwise). Records show it met acceptance criteria and who released it.

Common finding

Final inspection record not signed; release done before all checks were finished.

8.7Nonconforming outputs (NCRs and deviations)

Bad product is identified and controlled so it isn't used or shipped by mistake. It's corrected, segregated, returned, scrapped, or accepted under concession. Records describe the nonconformity, the action taken, any concession, and who decided.

Auditor does

Visits the quarantine area and picks a tagged part: "Show me the NCR and the decision." Asks for a recent deviation and checks it was approved by the right authority, and by the customer where the contract requires it.

Common finding

Rejected parts mixed with good ones; NCR open for months with no disposition; deviation signed by the wrong person; customer approval missing.

9 · Performance evaluation

9.1Monitoring, customer satisfaction, analysis

You decide what to measure and how, monitor customer perception, and analyse the data: product conformity, customer satisfaction, process performance, supplier performance, how well objectives are met, and whether risk actions worked.

Auditor asks

"What does the data tell you? What decisions came from it?"

Common finding

Data is collected but never analysed or acted on; no customer satisfaction measure (complaints alone usually aren't enough).

9.2Internal audit

A planned audit programme covering the QMS, based on importance, changes and past results. Auditors are objective (they don't audit their own work). Results are reported to management, and corrections and corrective actions are taken without undue delay.

Auditor asks you

"Show me the programme. How did you decide the frequency? Show me a report and a finding from it through to closure. Who audited Quality?"

Common finding

Areas skipped; auditor audited their own area; reports with no evidence detail ("all OK"); findings never closed.

Tip: internal audits that find real problems are a good sign to an external auditor. A year of "no findings" makes them suspicious.

9.3Management review

Top management reviews the QMS at planned intervals. The minutes must show these inputs were covered:

  • Status of actions from previous reviews
  • Changes in internal and external issues
  • Performance: customer satisfaction and feedback; objectives; process performance and product conformity; nonconformities and corrective actions; monitoring and measurement results; audit results; external provider performance
  • Adequacy of resources
  • Effectiveness of actions on risks and opportunities
  • Opportunities for improvement

And outputs: decisions on improvements, needed changes to the QMS, and resource needs.

Common finding

An input is missing (often supplier performance or risk effectiveness); no decisions recorded; actions from the last review not followed up.

10 · Improvement

10.2Nonconformity and corrective action

When something goes wrong (including complaints): react and correct, find the root cause, check whether similar problems exist or could happen elsewhere, act, review effectiveness, and update risks or the QMS if needed. Keep records.

Auditor does

Picks a closed corrective action and reads it end to end. Is the root cause real? Does the action address it? Is there evidence it was effective?

Common finding

Root cause written as "operator error" or "retrained operator"; closed without an effectiveness check; the same problem keeps coming back.

10.1 10.3Continual improvement 2026

Show that the system gets better over time, with examples and data: scrap down, cycle time down, fewer complaints, a recurring problem eliminated.

Auditor asks

"What has improved since recertification? Show me the before and after."

No clause matches that search. Try a simpler word, such as "gauge", "supplier" or "record".

In the room

How to answer, what they'll ask each group, and what to say when you're not sure.

Golden rules: share with everyone

  1. Listen to the whole question. Ask them to repeat it if needed.
  2. Answer what was asked, honestly and briefly. Don't add stories nobody asked for, but never hide anything.
  3. Show, don't tell. "Let me show you the record" beats any explanation.
  4. "I don't know" is a fine answer, followed by "…but I know where to find out" or "…[name] owns that."
  5. Never guess or make something up. One wrong answer makes the auditor dig deeper.
  6. Avoid "always" and "never" unless records prove it. The auditor will go looking for the one exception.
  7. Pausing is fine. Taking time to find the right record is normal.
  8. Correct yourself straight away if you got something wrong: "Sorry, I misspoke. It's a 12-month interval."
  9. Don't argue. If you disagree, ask which requirement it's against and show evidence calmly.

Problems you found yourself count in your favour

If the auditor finds something you already know about, show them the NCR, CAPA or internal audit finding. A problem caught and being fixed by your own system shows the system working. Auditors often won't raise a finding for something already under control.

Useful phrases

"Just to check I understand: you're asking how we control drawing revisions at the machine?" "Let me pull up the record rather than go from memory." "That's owned by our purchasing lead. I'll bring them in." "We picked that up in our own internal audit in June. Here's the corrective action." "Could you show me which requirement that's against, so we address it properly?" "Is that a nonconformity, or an observation?"

Follow the trail

Auditors test a system by following one item through linked records. The link at each step is an identifier (PO number, drawing revision, job number, gauge ID, heat number) that has to match the next record. If it doesn't match, that's usually where a finding comes from.

Findings and your response

How findings are graded, what happens next, and how to write a response the CB will accept.

How findings are graded

Major NC A nonconformity that affects the capability of the management system to achieve its intended results. Examples: a required process isn't there or isn't being done at all (no internal audits for a year, no management review); a breakdown likely to let bad product reach the customer. Several minors against the same requirement can add up to a major.

Minor NC A nonconformity that doesn't affect the system's capability, usually an isolated lapse: one overdue gauge, one unsigned record, one old drawing.

OFI / observation Not a nonconformity. Advice or a risk the auditor noticed. No formal response needed, but deal with it, or it could become a finding next time.

Many CBs raise a minor from the last audit as a major if it wasn't actually fixed. Check your CB's rules.

What happens next

  • Minor: you send a correction and corrective action plan, the CB reviews and accepts it, and the auditor checks implementation and effectiveness at the next audit.
  • Major: the CB must review and verify your correction and corrective action, by document review or a follow-up visit, often at extra cost. If a major isn't resolved in time, your certificate can be suspended.
  • Deadlines are set by your CB, not by ISO 9001. Check its certification rules or your contract. Commonly about 30 days to submit a response, with majors closed within about 90 days, but confirm at the closing meeting.

How to write the response

1
Finding. Copy the auditor's wording exactly, with the clause.
2
Correction and containment. What you fixed right away, and what you did about product that may have been affected.
3
Extent. Did the same thing happen elsewhere? Check other gauges, records, cells and suppliers, and say what you found.
4
Root cause. Why did the system allow it? Use 5 Whys or a fishbone. Keep asking until you reach something you can change.
5
Corrective action. A change to the system that removes that cause, with an owner and a date.
6
Evidence. Attach what proves it's done: revised procedure, screenshot, training record, photo.
7
Effectiveness check. How and when you'll confirm it worked, e.g. three months of data, or a follow-up internal audit.

Weak vs strong root cause

✗ Weak: "Operator error. Operator retrained."

Retraining is the right fix only if the training really was missing. Otherwise the auditor will ask why the system let one person's mistake through, and why it won't happen with the next person.

✓ Strong: "The drawing release process had no step to remove superseded copies from the floor. Printed copies stayed in the cell after an ECN."

This points at something you can change (the process), so the fix sticks no matter who is on shift.

Root-cause checker

Paste a root cause or corrective action before you send it to the CB. This checks for the wording auditors usually reject. It runs in this page only, and nothing is saved or sent.

Worked example: overdue gauge

Finding (minor, 7.1.5.2)

"A 1–2 in outside micrometer (ID OM-17) was in use in the turning cell, 2 months past its calibration due date."

Correction and containment

OM-17 removed from use the same day and calibrated. As-found readings were within tolerance, so no product impact; the calibration record is attached. (If it had been out of tolerance: list every job it measured since its last good calibration, assess the impact, and inform the customer if needed.)

Extent

All 140 gauges in the system checked against the recall list: 2 more found overdue (both in the tool crib, not in use). Both calibrated.

Root cause

The recall list was a spreadsheet maintained by one person, with no automatic reminder. When that person was on leave, the monthly recall was missed. Gauge labels showed a calibration date but not the due date, so operators couldn't check at the point of use.

Corrective action

Calibration system set to send a weekly due-in-30-days report to QA and the backup owner (owner: QA, done 2026-12-05). Labels changed to show the due date. Operators briefed to check the label before use, and the check added to the daily layered audit.

Effectiveness check

Zero overdue gauges in use across 12 weekly reports and the Q2 internal audit of 7.1.5.

Don't promise what you won't do

Every date in your response will be checked at the next audit. Choose actions you can really finish, with real owners. Missing your own CAPA dates can be raised as a new finding.
